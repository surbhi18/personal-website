## Course Description and Objectives

This advanced graduate-level course explores the theoretical foundations of large language models (LLMs). We will study the core components of modern LLMs—from pretraining and transformer architecture to prompting, reasoning, and reinforcement learning—through a mathematical and algorithmic lens. The topics follow the order of the modern LLM pipeline, so that we can ask, at each stage, what that stage adds to the model: new information, new computation, or neither.

We will discuss both positive and negative theoretical results, examining their assumptions and how well they explain models used in practice. Many of these results are proved for models and data that are much simpler than current LLMs, and understanding what such results do and do not say is a large part of what we will practice in this course. The goal is to equip you with the technical tools needed to read the recent literature, understand the fundamental capabilities and limitations of LLMs, and identify open research questions.

## Recommended Preparation

There are no official prerequisites. Students are expected to be comfortable with probability, linear algebra, calculus, and fundamental machine learning concepts, at the level of CIS 5200 or an equivalent course. Mathematical maturity to read research papers is important. Familiarity with deep learning and transformers is helpful but not required. Information theory, hidden-state models, automata, learning theory, and reinforcement learning will be introduced as needed.

## Class Format

Lectures will develop the core theoretical concepts from first principles, mostly on the board. Papers will be used to provide context and examples rather than as a substitute for the lectures. The course project will give you an opportunity to explore an open question through theoretical or empirical work.

## Requirements and Grading

1. **Homework (25%)**: One handwritten problem set based on the mathematics developed in lecture. It will include a short one-on-one discussion of your submission with me or a teaching assistant.
2. **Course Project (65%)**: The project must involve original research connected to one or more topics covered in the course. It can be theoretical, empirical, or a mix of the two. You may work individually or in pairs, and all project topics must be approved. You may build on your current research if the project is clearly connected to the course; the best outcome would be a manuscript suitable for submission to a machine learning conference or journal. The project has four components:
   * **Project proposal (5%)**: At most two pages of content, plus references. State the research question, explain why it is interesting, describe your approach, and discuss the most relevant prior work. Please be mindful of scope and avoid being overly ambitious.
   * **Mid-semester presentation (15%)**: A short presentation covering your progress so far, preliminary results, approaches that did not work, and your plan for the rest of the semester.
   * **Final presentation (25%)**: A presentation covering the problem, approach, main results, and conclusions. The format and time limit will depend on the number of projects.
   * **Project final report (20%)**: A report in the style of a NeurIPS paper, with at most six pages of content, plus references and appendices. You do not need to use all six pages.
3. **In-class Participation (10%)**: Based on attendance and contributions to class discussions.

## Syllabus and Readings

The list below is tentative. The balance among topics will depend on the pace of the course and on student interest. Lecture notes and the readings for each topic will be posted here as the semester progresses.

### Topics

1. **Next-token prediction and pretraining.** We will start with the objective itself: how a language model factorizes a distribution over sequences, what the log-loss measures, and why minimizing it on a large corpus is a sensible thing to do. We will then look at where that reasoning runs out, and why predicting each token well need not add up to a good sequence.
2. **Generation and hallucination.** Generating text that is new, correct, and varied has to be done from training data that only ever shows what belongs to a language and never what falls outside it. We will ask whether good generation is possible at all under that constraint, and why models that fit their training data well can still state things that are false.
3. **Transformer architecture and computation.** We will then fix a trained model and ask what attention-based architectures can compute at all, and how the answer depends on depth and input length. We will keep three questions apart throughout: whether a Transformer can represent a solution, whether training actually finds it, and whether that solution still works on inputs longer than the ones it was trained on.
4. **Prompting and in-context learning.** The same fixed weights behave differently depending on what the prompt contains. We will compare the main explanations for this, including inferring the task, retrieving similar examples, and running a learning algorithm inside the forward pass, and make explicit what each one assumes about the data.
5. **Reasoning with intermediate steps.** Still without touching the weights, we can let a model work on paper before it answers, and this reliably helps. We will ask where the gain comes from, whether it is the extra computation bought before committing to an answer or the extra supervision carried by worked examples in the training data, and what a longer chain makes solvable.
6. **Post-training from demonstrations and preferences.** The remaining topics change the model after pretraining, beginning with the case where all we have is a dataset collected in advance, either of examples to copy or of judgments about which of two answers is better. We will ask what such a dataset can and cannot change about a model, developing the standard answers along the way: fine-tuning as imitation, comparisons turned into a reward model, and the objectives behind RLHF and DPO.
7. **Reinforcement learning and self-improvement.** Replacing that fixed dataset with an online reward signal, we will ask what the reward adds beyond fine-tuning and how the base model limits what exploration can reach. We will then ask when a model can improve on its own generations, with no new external data at all.
8. **Test-time computation and verification.** Finally we will spend computation rather than data. The weights stay fixed here, as they did for prompting and chains of thought, but the effort now goes into producing many candidate answers and checking them, so we will ask how much repeated sampling, search, and verification can buy, at what cost, and what happens when the verifier itself is imperfect.

## Policies

We will primarily use Ed Discussion for course communication. Questions that may be useful to other students should be posted publicly. You can post privately on Ed to contact the instructor when needed.

### Homework Collaboration

Collaboration is permitted and encouraged on the homework, but each student must understand, write, and submit their own solutions. You may discuss the problems with other students, but you may not look at another student's written solutions while preparing your submission. You may not publicly post partial or complete solutions on Ed. If you collaborate with anyone, please list your collaborators on your submission.

### Late Work

If extenuating circumstances affect your ability to submit the homework or a project component on time, please contact the instructor privately on Ed as early as possible.

### Use of AI Tools

AI tools may be used for the homework and course project. This includes concept clarification, brainstorming, working through problems, editing, and code assistance. You are responsible for checking the correctness of all generated material and must understand everything that you submit. Each submission should include a statement describing how AI tools were used. You may be asked to explain your reasoning or discuss any part of your submission.
