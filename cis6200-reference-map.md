# CIS 6200: theory of large language models — reference map

_Working bibliography for Fall 2026. Last literature pass: August 14, 2026. This file is deliberately unpublished while the syllabus is being designed._

This is a course-design map, not a claim to contain every paper that could be called “LLM theory.” It favors work that tries to explain a behavior specific to modern language models: autoregressive prediction and generation, Transformer computation, in-context learning, scaling and data, reasoning traces, preference or reinforcement post-training, verification, and test-time computation. A generic neural-network or feature-learning paper belongs only when it directly resolves one of those questions.

## How to read the map

The **course-use** column is a recommendation, not a quality ranking:

- **Anchor** — a natural paper around which to organize a lecture or an entire question.
- **Strong** — a good companion, counterpoint, proof exercise, or student-presentation paper.
- **Watch** — recent, unsettled, narrowly scoped, or best assigned after its assumptions are made explicit.

Tags describe why a paper is here:

- **B — big idea:** creates a course-level question or supplies a reusable explanatory lens around which several later papers can be organized. A useful test is whether the paper can sustain a lecture on the problem formulation, tool, result, and limitations—not merely report a sharper bound or a better method. For a quick view of the formal big-idea papers, filter mentally for **B + R**; a **B** without **R** is usually a problem-setting empirical or conceptual paper.
- **F — foundational:** an early or canonical problem formulation, empirical phenomenon, or field-defining result. This is not a claim of uncontested historical priority.
- **T — teachable:** has a clean theorem, construction, experiment, or tension that can be explained in one class segment. This tag is intentionally selective.
- **R — rigorous:** the principal contribution is a theorem or formal characterization.
- **M — mechanistic:** proposes or identifies a concrete mechanism, often in a controlled model.
- **E — empirical:** establishes a phenomenon, law, or careful empirical correction that theory should explain.
- **N — new frontier:** work from roughly 2024–2026. “New” is not an endorsement; publication status and assumptions matter.

Venue labels distinguish archival publications from preprints and submissions wherever that status could affect how strongly the course presents a claim. To keep the map scannable, author lists are omitted here; each title links to the archival record or author preprint containing the full citation.

**Snapshot:** 208 papers: 99 **Anchor**, 78 **Strong**, and 31 **Watch**. Of these, 63 are marked **B** (42 also carry **R**), 65 are marked **F**, 99 are marked **T**, 100 are marked **R**, and 100 are from the 2024–2026 frontier. The list is intentionally much wider than one semester; roughly 16 papers become core or selected-section readings, with the rest serving as counter-readings, lecture references, and project seeds.

## Student-facing course narrative

The course follows one question from pretraining through deployment: **what information and computation does each stage add?** The order below is a dependency order, not a chronology of the literature.

### Part I: What does pretraining identify?

1. **Autoregressive prediction and data support.** Begin with conditional probabilities, log loss, teacher forcing, decoding, and rollout. **Bridge:** local prediction defines a sequence distribution, but it does not by itself specify useful generation.
2. **Generation, breadth, hallucination, and abstention.** Separate prediction, identification, and generation; then introduce validity, breadth, missing mass, and calibration. **Bridge:** these limits do not depend on a Transformer, so they should be understood before studying the architecture.

### Part II: What can a fixed model compute or infer?

3. **Transformer computation and learned algorithms.** Move from explicit attention programs to depth and precision limits, then from representability to learned shortcuts and length extrapolation. **Bridge:** after identifying what a model should predict, ask how a finite architecture carries out the computation.
4. **In-context adaptation.** Compare latent-task inference, retrieval, and implemented learning algorithms without treating any one controlled model as a universal explanation. **Bridge:** prompts provide new information while weights remain fixed.
5. **Reasoning with intermediate steps.** Separate trace length as serial computation, intermediate supervision as information, and a visible trace as explanation. **Bridge:** a model may use its context not only as data but also as writable computational state.

### Part III: Where can improvement after pretraining come from?

6. **Post-training from demonstrations and preferences.** Progress from supervised fine-tuning to pairwise reward learning, KL-regularized control, and direct preference optimization under a fixed dataset. **Bridge:** offline feedback changes weights but cannot choose which new responses receive labels.
7. **Reinforcement learning and self-improvement.** Introduce on-policy reward data, exploration, coverage, and sharpening. **Bridge:** online interaction can change the data distribution; self-improvement without external information can only exploit a generation–verification gap already present in the model.
8. **Fixed-weight computation and verification.** Progress from repeated sampling to imperfect outcome verification and then process verification with backtracking. **Bridge:** compare training-time amortization with inference-time search while accounting for verifier provenance and cost.

Every lecture uses the same five-part audit developed below: identify the **object**, **information source**, **computational resource**, **claim type**, and **evaluation distribution**. The recurring distinctions are prediction versus generation, representability versus acquisition, weight-changing improvement versus fixed-weight search, and additional computation versus additional information.

### Tentative 24-meeting spine

1. Predictor, sequence distribution, decoder, policy, reward model, and verifier.
2. Log loss, teacher forcing, rollout distributions, and the NTP–behavior-cloning tension.
3. Data support, rarity, dependence, realizability, and misspecification.
4. Prediction versus identification versus generation; validity, novelty, and breadth.
5. Missing mass, calibration, and unavoidable factual errors.
6. Decoding, abstention, retrieval, negative feedback, and what changes an impossibility.
7. The decoder-only Transformer as a mathematical object.
8. Attention programs, retrieval, aggregation, and parallel composition.
9. Limits under explicit depth, precision, position, masking, and uniformity assumptions.
10. Learned shortcuts, identifiability, and length extrapolation.
11. Formalizing tasks, demonstrations, queries, retrieval, and task inference.
12. In-context learning as latent-variable Bayesian inference.
13. Implemented learning algorithms, induction mechanisms, and synthetic-to-LLM caveats.
14. Intermediate tokens as serial computation.
15. Intermediate supervision and the learnability of autoregressive traces.
16. Useful scratchpads versus faithful explanations.
17. Supervised fine-tuning, pairwise comparisons, and reward-model estimation.
18. KL-regularized control, the reward–policy correspondence, and DPO.
19. Offline coverage, distribution shift, Goodhart effects, and reward overoptimization.
20. Online reward data, exploration, and the computational role of base-model coverage.
21. Self-improvement, sharpening, and training-time amortization of verification.
22. Repeated sampling, majority vote, best-of-\(N\), and candidate coverage.
23. Imperfect outcome rewards, reward hacking, and pessimistic selection.
24. Process verification, error amplification, stochastic backtracking, and the final information audit.

## Big-idea theory core

This is a depth-first intellectual spine, not a week-by-week schedule. Each unit begins with a precise question, teaches a reusable tool, and stages a disagreement or limitation. Papers marked **B** are candidates for this core; the rest of the map supplies counter-readings, proof exercises, and project paths.

| Course question | Big-idea reading arc | Main tools or explanatory lenses | What students should learn to interrogate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What does next-token prediction actually optimize? | *Language Modeling Is Compression* → *Auto-Regressive NTPs are Universal Learners* ↔ *Pitfalls of NTP* | Source coding; autoregressive trace constructions; statistical–computational separations; teacher forcing | Distinguish an objective's representational power from what finite data and optimization recover. |
| Is generation a different learning problem from prediction? | *Language Generation in the Limit* → *Generation through the Lens of Learning Theory* ↔ *On the Limits of Language Generation* → *Density Measures* | Gold–Angluin identification; adversarial generation games; closure dimension; density and topology | Define validity and breadth before claiming that a generator has learned a language. |
| Is hallucination avoidable, and can it be detected? | *Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate* → *No Free Lunch* / *Accuracy Incentivizes Hallucinations* → *Innovation*; *Automated Hallucination Detection* as a second impossibility | Good–Turing missing mass; VC dimension; calibration and abstention incentives; reductions to language identification | Separate inevitability from incentives and detection, and identify exactly which feedback changes an impossibility result. |
| What can a Transformer compute? | *Turing Completeness* ↔ *Theoretical Limitations of Self-Attention* / *Saturated Transformers* → *Turing Completeness of Prompting* | Simulation; precision and uniformity; formal-language witnesses; circuit complexity; universal interpreters | Reconcile apparently contradictory theorems by auditing depth, precision, memory, length, and output assumptions. |

| Why does in-context learning appear? | *Few-Shot Learners* → *Implicit Bayesian Inference* / *Transformers Learn by Gradient Descent* → *Learnability of ICL* / *Occam's Razor* | Latent-variable Bayesian inference; explicit optimizer constructions; PAC sample complexity; prequential coding and MDL | Ask which data-generating assumptions make each ICL explanation valid and whether it transfers to pretrained LLMs. |
| What mechanism carries and composes an in-context rule? | *Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits* → *Induction Heads* / *Statistical Induction Heads* → *One-Layer Lower Bound* / *\(k\)-Hop Induction* ↔ *Function Vectors* | QK/OV composition; conditional \(k\)-grams; communication complexity; pointer doubling; causal intervention | Distinguish higher-order Markov memory from multi-hop composition, and a clean synthetic circuit from the dominant mechanism in a deployed model. |
| Why are scaling curves smooth while skills look discrete? | *Scaling Laws* → *Chinchilla*; *Emergent Abilities* ↔ *Mirage?* → *Quantization Model* | Power laws; constrained compute allocation; metric transformations; heavy-tailed skill-frequency models | Determine whether a threshold belongs to the model, the task distribution, or the evaluation metric. |
| How do data structure and recursion shape what an LM learns? | *DoReMi* / *Theory of Language Structure* → *Counterfactual Memorization* → *AI Models Collapse* | Group DRO; PCFG correlations; causal leave-one-document-out effects; recursive distribution dynamics | Treat mixture, hierarchy, duplication, dependence, and synthetic recursion as structure—not just token count. |
| Does chain of thought change computation, learning, or only observability? | *CoT Prompting* → *Why Think Step by Step?* → *Expressive Power with CoT* / *Theory of Learning with CoT* ↔ *Unfaithful Explanations* | Scratchpad length as computational time; locality; complexity classes; latent versus observed traces; causal interventions | Separate improved answers, added compute, changed sample complexity, and faithful explanation. |
| What mathematical object is post-training optimizing? | *DPO* → *General Theoretical Paradigm* → *Reward Overoptimization* / *Online Data via Coverage* → *Good Foundation?* / *RL after NTP* | KL-regularized control and inverse policy maps; \(\Psi\)PO; Goodhart curves; concentrability; sampling oracles and exploration | State whether gains come from preference estimation, online coverage, exploration, or reweighting behavior already supported by the base model. |
| When do verification and test-time compute create new capability? | *Process- vs Outcome-Based Feedback* → *Query Complexity of Verifier-Assisted Generation* → *Optimal Test-Time Compute* → *Sharpening* | Generator and verifier oracles; query complexity and backtracking; compute frontiers; generation–verification gaps | Account separately for candidate generation, verification quality, search policy, and the source of any new information. |

### A repeatable deep-dive format

For each course question, assign at most two **B** papers as required reading—usually one that creates the question and one that supplies the formal lens—plus a short counter-reading. Ask students to reconstruct the same five-part audit every time:

1. **Object:** What exactly is being modeled—a predictor, distribution, generator, prompted system, verifier, or policy?
2. **Information:** What observations or feedback enter through pretraining data, a prompt, demonstrations, comparisons, rewards, retrieval, or verification?
3. **Resource:** What is being spent—samples, model depth, arithmetic precision, intermediate tokens, online interactions, or inference queries—and which mathematical tool analyzes it?
4. **Guarantee:** Is the result an existence theorem, learning bound, optimization result, lower bound, characterization, mechanism, or empirical law?
5. **Evaluation and boundary:** On which distribution is the claim measured, which assumption does the most work, and which counter-reading shows its limit?

This keeps a lecture from becoming a sequence of paper summaries: the students learn a vocabulary for comparing explanations across topics.

## 10. Formal theories of language generation

These papers ask what it means to generate valid, novel, and sufficiently broad outputs before proposing an algorithm. Their abstractions do not model every detail of a deployed LLM; their value is to expose which goals are compatible, which feedback changes the answer, and how generation differs from prediction or identification.

## Deliberate omissions and boundary cases

- Generic optimization, neural tangent kernel, and feature-learning theory is omitted unless the result explains an LLM-specific behavior.
- Architecture-system papers are included only when they define the object being theorized about; this is not intended to become a standard deep-learning architecture unit.
- Benchmark-only papers are omitted unless they establish a phenomenon that generated a substantial theoretical question.
- Mechanistic-interpretability papers are included selectively when they make a causal or algorithmic claim, not merely because they visualize activations.
- Publication status is always shown. Recent preprints are generally **Watch**; a field-defining technical report may still be an **Anchor**, but its non-archival status remains visible.
